# Modality layer: a map

Status: draft map for review, 2026-10-11. This is not a course outline. It lists candidate modality courses for yoga, Pilates and sport development, so that the base modules and claim work can be planned around them.

## What the modality layer is

- **Layer:** `modality` ([ARCHITECTURE.md](../../docs/ARCHITECTURE.md)). Spindle writes and owns these courses. All tenants see them, read-only. Gyms add their own `method` courses on top. Those are always practice-based and live only in the gym's own tenant.
- **Support:** a block is *Science* only where an admissible source supports it: a systematic review, meta-analysis, position stand, consensus statement, guideline or care pathway (D-04, D-12). Everything else is *Practice-based*. The no-downgrade rule from the base outlines applies: a science statement with no approved claim is removed or rewritten as a coaching recommendation. It never keeps its content under a practice label.
- **Builds on the base.** A modality course refers back to base lessons for anatomy, loading and referral. It never restates a base claim with new wording. It binds the same claim.
- **Style and safety:** [STYLE.md](../../docs/curriculum/STYLE.md) and the base safety rules (SAFETY.md and the hip rows in `content/hip/OUTLINE.md`). Each modality adds its own safety notes below.
- **Originality (D-10):** no coach or school names, no endorsements, no copied curriculum structure, sequence, cue list or programme from any teacher, school or certifying body. Classical exercise and posture names are used only as common vocabulary.
- **Contested frameworks** appear only as practice-based context, with a note that the evidence is limited (STYLE.md › Evidence). Examples: energy or chakra models, "core" models that make causal claims, and claims of flexibility "lengthening fascia".

**How to read the course tables**
- "Base links" names the base modules and claim slots a course leans on. `shoulder:l6-progression` means the shoulder module's `claim-slot:l6-progression`.
- "Candidate claims" are research leads. Europe PMC confirmed that each record exists on 2026-10-11, and the statements were checked against abstracts only. Licences are as Europe PMC reported them. "None stated" means Europe PMC listed no licence.
- **Base modules that do not exist yet** are named as gaps: spine and trunk, knee, ankle and foot. A course that needs them waits for them.

**Candidate claims**
- **Y1. Yoga versus no exercise for back pain.** In adults with chronic non-specific low back pain, at three months, yoga probably gives an improvement in pain that is not clinically important (moderate certainty), and may give a small improvement in back-related function that is also not clinically important (low certainty), compared with no exercise.
  - Source: Wieland LS, et al. Yoga for chronic non-specific low back pain. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2022;11:CD010671. PMID 36398843, PMC9673466, doi:10.1002/14651858.cd010671.pub3. Licence: none stated.
  - Evidence: Cochrane systematic review, 21 trials, 2,223 participants.
- **Y2. Yoga versus other exercise.** Compared with other back-focused exercise, yoga probably makes little or no difference to back-related function at 3 months (moderate certainty). For pain the evidence is very uncertain.
  - Source: the same review as Y1.
- **Y3. Adverse events.** At 6 to 12 months, compared with no exercise, yoga may increase adverse events, mostly more back pain (low certainty). Compared with back-focused exercise, yoga may make little or no difference to the risk of adverse events (low certainty).
  - Source: the same review as Y1.
- **Y4. Balance and mobility over 60.** In people aged 60 and over, yoga-based exercise gave a small improvement in balance and a medium improvement in mobility. Whether it prevents falls is not known.
  - Source: Youkhana S, et al. Yoga-based exercise improves balance and mobility in people aged 60 and over: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Age Ageing* 2016;45(1):21-29. PMID 26707903, doi:10.1093/ageing/afv175. Licence: none stated.
  - Evidence: 6 trials, 307 participants.
- **Y5. Fitness in older adults.** Yoga showed moderate improvements in muscle strength, balance, mobility and lower-body flexibility, with no significant effect on endurance or upper-body flexibility.
  - Source: Shin S. Meta-analysis of the effect of yoga practice on physical fitness in the elderly. *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 2021;18(21):11663. PMID 34770176, PMC8583600, doi:10.3390/ijerph182111663. Licence: CC BY.
  - Evidence: meta-analysis of 12 studies, with no certainty rating in the abstract.
- **Y6. Reported hazards (a list, not rates).** A review of published case reports found that musculoskeletal events were the largest group (40%), followed by eye events. Headstand was the most often implicated practice, followed by forceful breathing practices (pranayama). Many cases had pre-existing conditions.
  - Source: Sharma S, et al. Yoga and its risks: a systematic review of adverse events reported in case reports and case series. *J Integr Med* 2026;24(5):667-682. PMID 42471295, doi:10.1016/j.joim.2026.07.001. Licence: none stated.
  - Limit: case reports give no incidence and do not show cause. The block must not imply a risk rate or that a posture caused harm, and must not become a list of banned postures.

**Weak or missing evidence (practice-based):**
- strength and flexibility gains in younger healthy adults;
- style comparisons;
- breathing techniques.

A 2025 systematic review in healthy adults found low-certainty balance gains, with mixed results on falls (Bajaj P, et al. *Osteoporos Int* 2025;36(2):193-224; PMID 39607489). It is kept as background only.

**Safety notes for a yoga course**
- **Pregnancy.**
  - The 2019 Canadian guideline supports physical activity throughout an uncomplicated pregnancy, with identified contraindications (Mottola MF, et al. *Br J Sports Med* 2018;52(21):1339-1346; PMID 30337460, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2018-100056; licence none stated).
  - We found no admissible source that lists unsafe yoga postures in pregnancy.
  - So posture-level advice stays practice-based, and a pregnant client with symptoms is referred. The base modules exclude pregnancy and the first year after birth: with pelvic girdle or groin pain they refer (hip §2), and without it, training in pregnancy or postpartum is outside the base until reviewed pregnancy and postpartum safety rows exist (Gaps, item 3). A pregnancy course would be its own modality course, only after those rows.
- **Screening.** Y6 supports screening for pre-existing conditions. Eye conditions (for example glaucoma) and inverted postures need a referral rule. **GAP:** no admissible source read for that rule yet, so it starts as Spindle policy.
- **Base safety.** All base stop rules and tiers apply unchanged inside a yoga session.

| P-C | Pilates and balance for older adults | modality | Science (P3, plus the negative finding P4); practice-based for set-ups | hip: l2, l4; hip §3 and §4 |
| P-D | Mat and apparatus: loading, range and progression | modality | Practice-based, except where base loading claims apply | shoulder: l6-progression; hip: l6-glute-hypertrophy, l6-full-range |
| P-E | After childbirth: returning to a Pilates class | modality | Science only for pelvic floor training (P6); otherwise practice-based, with referral rules | Blocked until reviewed pregnancy and postpartum safety rows exist (Gaps, item 3); hip §2 refers pelvic girdle or groin pain |

**Candidate claims**
- **P1. Pilates versus other exercise for back pain.** For chronic non-specific low back pain, comparisons of Pilates with other exercise are low to very low certainty. The authors advise choosing by the person's goals and preferences.
  - Source: Wong CM, Rugg B, Geere JA. The effects of Pilates exercise in comparison to other forms of exercise on pain and disability in individuals with chronic non-specific low back pain: a systematic review with meta-analysis. *Musculoskeletal Care* 2023;21(1):78-96. PMID 36912214, doi:10.1002/msc.1667. Licence: none stated.
  - Evidence: 11 randomised trials.
- **P2. Pilates versus minimal intervention.** Compared with minimal intervention, Pilates reduced pain and disability in people with low back pain (low- to moderate-quality evidence). Compared with other exercise, there was no clear difference in disability.
  - Source: Yamato TP, et al. Pilates for low back pain: complete republication of a Cochrane review. *Spine* 2016;41(12):1013-1021. PMID 26679894, doi:10.1097/brs.0000000000001398. Licence: none stated.
  - Evidence: 10 trials. Searches ran to 2014, so prefer a newer review when one is admitted.
  - Background only: Patti A, et al. *Disabil Rehabil* 2024 (PMID 37632387). The abstract reports benefit over no exercise but gives no certainty rating.
- **P3. Balance in older adults.** In older adults, Pilates improved static and dynamic balance compared with control groups, and was comparable to other forms of exercise. It did not reduce the number of falls or fear of falling. Certainty ranged from very low to moderate, and no outcome reached high certainty.
  - Source: de Campos Júnior JF, et al. Effects of Pilates exercises on postural balance and reduced risk of falls in older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Complement Ther Clin Pract* 2024;57:101888. PMID 39068875, doi:10.1016/j.ctcp.2024.101888. Licence: none stated.
  - Evidence: 39 studies, 1,770 participants.
- **P4. Strength, endurance and power in older adults (insufficient evidence).** The authors conclude that Pilates cannot currently be recommended to improve strength, endurance or power in older adults. The outcomes differ:
  - Strength: no significant improvement compared with control (low quality), and no difference compared with other exercise (moderate quality).
  - Endurance: some improvement, in the upper limbs compared with control and the lower limbs compared with other exercise (very low quality).
  - Power: too few studies to pool.
  - Source: Oliveira LS, et al. Effects of pilates exercises on strength, endurance and muscle power in older adults: systematic review and meta-analysis. *J Bodyw Mov Ther* 2024;39:615-634. PMID 38876695, doi:10.1016/j.jbmt.2024.02.021. Licence: none stated.
  - Evidence: 24 trials. The course uses this to point clients who want strength to the base loading lessons.
- **P5. Healthy adults.** In healthy adults, 5 to 12 weeks of Pilates two to three times a week improved abdominal endurance compared with no exercise, with no clear change in flexibility.
  - Source: Campos RR, et al. Effect of the Pilates method on physical conditioning of healthy subjects: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *J Sports Med Phys Fitness* 2016;56(7-8):864-873. PMID 26004043. No DOI listed. Licence: none stated.
  - Evidence: 9 trials. Searches ran to 2014.
- **P6. Pelvic floor training after childbirth.** In the first year after giving birth, pelvic floor muscle training reduced the odds of urinary incontinence compared with control groups (moderate certainty). Abdominal muscle training gave a greater reduction in the gap between the abdominal muscles than no exercise (low certainty). The review is not about Pilates. The course uses it only to say that pelvic floor training is supported, and refers the rest to a clinician.
  - Source: Beamish NF, et al. Impact of postpartum exercise on pelvic floor disorders and diastasis recti abdominis: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Br J Sports Med* 2025;59(8):562-575. PMID 39694630, PMC12013572, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2024-108619. Licence: CC BY-NC.

**Caveat for every Pilates claim:** trials often describe their Pilates programmes incompletely, so "Pilates" in research is not one standard dose (Barros BS, et al. *Disabil Rehabil* 2022;44(11):2428-2436; PMID 33096012, doi:10.1080/09638288.2020.1836269; a review of reporting quality). This source is used as context only. It is not a systematic review of effects.

**Weak or missing evidence (practice-based):**
- apparatus versus mat;
- the classical principles;
- terminology;
- posture-level postpartum advice.

**Safety notes for a Pilates course**
- **After childbirth.** Pelvic floor symptoms, abdominal separation, or pain in the pelvis or groin need a clinician before loading progresses (Spindle policy, supported in part by P6).
- **Pregnancy.** As for yoga.
- **Base safety.** Base stop rules and tiers apply. Loaded spinal flexion is not taught until the spine and trunk base module, with its own safety rows, exists (Gaps, item 1). When it is taught, it is an option chosen by tolerance, never called "unsafe". No source supports a ban, so a ban would be an absolute rule (STYLE.md › Anti-patterns).

## Sport development (coaches of youth athletes)

**Youth rule.** Sport development teaches adult coaches and trainers about youth athletes.
- Spindle's users are adults.
- No one under 18 is a user, a learner or a client record in the app.
- No youth data is stored.
- No feature invites youth participation.

Courses use wholly synthetic teaching scenarios only, never real or identifiable athletes. The child and adolescent referral rules (SAFETY §2 for the shoulder, hip §2 for the hip, thigh and knee) apply in every lesson.

| S-F | Safeguarding for coaches of minors | modality | Science, bound to approved claims, only from the IOC consensus statements (S11, S12); practice-based for local procedures, which follow the gym's own policy | none |

**Candidate claims**
- **S1. Youth resistance training consensus.** An international consensus position statement on youth resistance training exists.
  - Source: Lloyd RS, et al. Position statement on youth resistance training: the 2014 International Consensus. *Br J Sports Med* 2014;48(7):498-505. PMID 24055781, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2013-092952. Licence: none stated.
  - **The abstract holds no findings,** so no claim can be staged until the full text is read (D-13 excerpt or full text).
- **S2. Youth athletic development consensus.** The IOC consensus calls for one evidence-informed approach to developing healthy, resilient young athletes, at every level of participation. Specific recommendations need the full text.
  - Source: Bergeron MF, et al. International Olympic Committee consensus statement on youth athletic development. *Br J Sports Med* 2015;49(13):843-851. PMID 26084524, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2015-094962. Licence: none stated.
- **S3. Specialising and injury.** Youth athletes who specialised in one sport had a higher injury risk than those who sampled several sports. This is an association from observational studies.
  - Source: Carder SL, et al. The concept of sport sampling versus sport specialization: preventing youth athlete injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Am J Sports Med* 2020;48(11):2850-2857. PMID 31961703, doi:10.1177/0363546519899380. Licence: none stated.
  - Evidence: 6 studies, 5,736 athletes aged 7 to 18.
- **S4. Specialisation consensus.** A consensus found no evidence that early specialisation benefits young children in most sports. It named overuse injury and burnout as risks.
  - Source: LaPrade RF, et al. AOSSM early sport specialization consensus statement. *Orthop J Sports Med* 2016;4(4):2325967116644241. PMID 27169132, PMC4853833, doi:10.1177/2325967116644241. Licence: CC BY-NC-ND.
- **S5. A definition.** A Delphi panel defined sport specialisation as intentional, focused participation in one sport for most of the year, which restricts other sports.
  - Source: Bell DR, et al. Consensus definition of sport specialization in youth athletes using a Delphi approach. *J Athl Train* 2021;56(11):1239-1251. PMID 33787895, PMC8582622, doi:10.4085/1062-6050-0725.20. Licence: none stated.
- **S6. A structured warm-up for young footballers.** In young football players, the FIFA11+ Kids warm-up roughly halved overall injury risk compared with a normal warm-up. The programme is named as research context only.
  - Source: Yang J, et al. Effects of the "FIFA11+ Kids" program on injury prevention in children: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 2022;19(19):12044. PMID 36231344, PMC9566496, doi:10.3390/ijerph191912044. Licence: CC BY.
  - Evidence: 6 studies, with no certainty rating in the abstract.
  - The course names the programme only as the review's title does. It does not reproduce the programme.
- **S7. Ankle injury prevention.** Exercise programmes reduced ankle injuries in athletes aged 13 to 19. Multi-component programmes with high adherence did best.
  - Source: Berkey R, et al. Ankle injury prevention programs for youth sports: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Sports Health* 2024;16(6):1029-1037. PMID 38406839, PMC11531064, doi:10.1177/19417381241231588. Licence: CC BY-NC.
- **S8. Implementation.** Workshops with supporting resources helped teams adopt neuromuscular warm-ups. Time and buy-in were the main barriers, and few studies tested real-world effect.
  - Source: Lutz D, et al. Best practices for the dissemination and implementation of neuromuscular training injury prevention warm-ups in youth team sport: a systematic review. *Br J Sports Med* 2024;58(11):615-625. PMID 38684329, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2023-106906. Licence: none stated.
- **S9. Growth and injury (very low certainty).** In male elite adolescent footballers, injuries may be more frequent around the growth spurt. The certainty of evidence is very low.
  - Source: Pakarinen O, Ponkilainen VT, Kuitunen I. *Health Sci Rep* 2025;8(7):e71096. PMID 40692568, PMC12277657, doi:10.1002/hsr2.71096. Licence: CC BY-NC.
  - Teach it hedged and football-only, or leave it out.
- **S10. Training load (evidence lacking).** In male elite youth footballers aged 12 to 21, evidence for a link between training load and injury is scarce.
  - Source: Verstappen S, et al. *Sports Med Open* 2021;7(1):6. PMID 33428001, PMC7801562, doi:10.1186/s40798-020-00296-1. Licence: CC BY.
  - The course must not teach load thresholds.
- **S11. Safeguarding consensus.** The IOC consensus on interpersonal violence and safeguarding calls for a whole-of-system approach.
  - Source: Tuakli-Wosornu YA, et al. IOC consensus statement: interpersonal violence and safeguarding in sport. *Br J Sports Med* 2024;58(22):1322-1344. PMID 39586634, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2024-108766. Licence: none stated.
- **S12. Harassment and abuse consensus.** The IOC consensus on harassment and abuse names child athletes among the groups at highest risk.
  - Source: Mountjoy M, et al. International Olympic Committee consensus statement: harassment and abuse (non-accidental violence) in sport. *Br J Sports Med* 2016;50(17):1019-1029. PMID 27118273, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2016-096121. Licence: none stated.

**Weak or missing evidence (practice-based or omitted):**
- load thresholds;
- growth-phase training rules;
- specific supervision ratios;
- youth lifting loads until S1's full text is read.

**Safety notes for a sport-development course**
- **Referral first.** A young athlete with shoulder, hip, thigh or knee pain follows the child and adolescent rows in SAFETY §2 and hip §2: stop, refer at the right tier, and tell a parent or carer. Pain elsewhere has no reviewed tier yet, so wider youth-pain referral rows are a prerequisite for any sport-development course that covers other regions (Gaps, item 5).
- **Safeguarding is not clinical advice.** S-F teaches recognition and reporting through the gym's own policy and local authorities. It never asks a coach to investigate.
- **No real or identifiable youth data, ever,** including in examples, screenshots or test fixtures. Wholly synthetic teaching scenarios are allowed.

## Gaps that block or shape these courses

1. **New base modules, each with its own safety rows:** spine and trunk, including the neck (needed by Y-B, P-B, Y-D's neck adaptations and any loaded spinal flexion), wrist (Y-D), knee, ankle and foot (S-C).
2. **Full texts:** S1 and S2 need full-text review (D-13) before any science block.
3. **Pregnancy and postpartum:** this needs its own safety rows and an admissible guideline beyond the Canadian guideline before any course covers it.
4. **Referral sources:** a source for eye conditions and inverted postures (yoga), and a specialist hip pathway (hip outline).
5. **Youth pain beyond the shoulder and hip:** reviewed child and adolescent referral rows for other regions before a sport-development course covers them.

## Open questions for review

1. Should sport development start with S-B and S-C, which have the strongest admissible evidence, before S-A and S-D, which need full texts?
2. The yoga and Pilates back-pain courses (Y-B, P-B) wait for the spine and trunk base module, its approved foundation claims and its safety rows. Is that the right order relative to the knee and ankle modules?
3. Is a safeguarding course (S-F) in Spindle's scope at all, or should it point only to national safeguarding bodies?
