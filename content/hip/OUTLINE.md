# Hip and pelvis module: outline

Status: draft outline for review, 2026-10-11. Base layer, the second base module after the shoulder. Eight lessons for working personal trainers. Text is ours (CC BY-SA 4.0). Style follows [STYLE.md](../../docs/curriculum/STYLE.md). Safety follows the rules of [SAFETY.md](../../docs/curriculum/SAFETY.md), applied to the hip through the hip safety rows below.

Nothing here is a lesson yet. Drafting waits for this outline's review, a hip safety document (see "Safety before drafting"), and claim dry runs for the slots below.

## How to read this outline

The labels match the shoulder outline:

- **[S]** a *Science* block. It needs an approved claim before it is published. "Candidate" names where we expect to look. Nothing is cited until the claim pipeline has verified the source.
- **[P]** a *Practice-based* block: coaching experience, cues, set-ups and progressions. It carries no claim and must not read like one.
- **No downgrades.** If a science candidate gets no approved claim, the statement is removed, or rewritten as a coaching recommendation that says nothing about how the body works or what research shows. It never keeps its content under a practice-based label.
- **[!]** a safety block, placed where the risk is. "Hip §n" refers to the hip safety rows at the end of this outline until they move into a hip safety document.
- **Graded quiz** candidates are science-only, and each binds a claim. **Scenario** candidates stay ungraded until the claims they need are approved.
- **Video slots** name a topic only. Channels and segments are admitted under D-09 and D-10, never named here.

Every section follows the shoulder pattern: *what it is*, *why it matters*, *how to see it*, *what to do*.

**Source rules (D-04, D-12).**
- **Foundation claims** (names, parts, attachments, relations) may rest on an open anatomy reference. Its license must be exactly CC BY, CC0 or public domain: `isOpenLicense` in `packages/evidence` enforces this.
- **Applied claims** (effects, risks, prevalence, associations) need a systematic review, meta-analysis, position stand, consensus statement, guideline or care pathway. Their license does not matter, because we only paraphrase them. It is recorded anyway.
- **Gaps** are marked **GAP**. A gap is never filled with practice wording.

**Claim slots.** Each slot below gives:
- an id;
- a working statement, which the claim dry run will tighten against the full text;
- a domain (foundation or applied);
- an evidence level;
- the population the evidence covers;
- candidate sources, with PMID, PMCID, DOI and license as Europe PMC reported them on 2026-10-11.

These rows are research leads. Europe PMC confirmed that each record exists. The working statements were checked against abstracts, not full texts.

## 1. Hip and pelvis anatomy a trainer needs

**One idea:** the hip joint joins the thigh bone to the pelvis. A capsule and three ligaments wrap it. Large muscle groups cross it in every direction.

**Objectives**
- Name the bones and joints of the hip and pelvis in plain words and in standard terms.
- Describe the capsule and the three ligaments that reinforce it.
- Name the muscle groups that cross the hip, and where each group sits.
- Find the main landmarks on yourself and, with permission, on a client.

**Sections**
1. *What it is: the capsule.* [S, foundation] Three ligaments reinforce the hip capsule: the iliofemoral, pubofemoral and ischiofemoral ligaments. A ring of fibers around the femoral neck (the zona orbicularis) is part of the capsule. The block uses names and relations only. Ng 2019 is surgical in focus, so its mechanics and surgery content stays out.
2. *What it is: the gluteus medius and the capsule's attachment.* [S, foundation] Gluteus medius has several parts. The capsule attaches more widely on the front and upper rim of the socket than older texts show. Only the structural description is used, not the authors' stability interpretation.
3. *What it is: the bones, the socket, and the joints of the pelvis.* **GAP.** The bones are the pelvis (ilium, ischium, pubis), the sacrum and the femur. The joints are the hip, the sacroiliac joints and the pubic symphysis. The socket (acetabulum) and its rim (labrum) belong here too. We found no plain-CC-BY anatomy reference for these. Without one, the statements are removed (no downgrade). Next sources to try:
   - an open nomenclature reference (TA2 via Wikidata or Uberon, D-12 `nomenclature_reference`);
   - another CC BY anatomy review.
4. *What it is: the muscle groups.* **GAP** for anatomy outside gluteus medius: gluteus maximus, iliopsoas, the adductors and the deep rotators. The candidates we found are CC BY-NC, carry no stated license, or are animal studies. Without an admissible source, the section is removed. The lesson must not describe muscle actions in any case, because actions are function, not foundation.
5. *Why it matters.* [P] Load the range a client can control.
6. *How to see it.* [P] A landmark tour, done on yourself first: the front of the pelvis at the hip bone, the top of the pelvis, and the bony point at the outside of the hip. Ask before you touch a client and say what you are touching. Offer to let the client find each landmark themselves. Never palpate the groin or the sit bones on a client; describe those landmarks instead (Spindle policy).
7. *What to do.* [P] Use plain names with clients and standard names with clinicians.
8. [!] The lesson teaches location, never what is "wrong". A painful spot is described, not named as an injury (SAFETY §1).

## 2. How the hip and pelvis move

**One idea:** the hip moves in every direction, and the pelvis and lower back share the work. How far a hip moves varies between people.

**Objectives**
- Name the six hip movements (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal and external rotation) and show each one.
- Explain that pelvic position changes the hip range available, and say for whom that evidence holds.
- Describe how hip range differs between people, including average sex differences.

**Sections**
1. *What it is: the movements.* [P] The six movements, shown standing and lying down. This is teaching vocabulary, not a claim.
2. *Pelvis and hip together.* [S] In people with femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), tilting the pelvis backward is associated with more hip motion before impingement. (The same review's dysplasia studies reported something different, increased joint stress with posterior tilt, so the claim is limited to FAI.) The block must state the population and must not generalize to healthy clients.
3. *Range varies.* [S] On average, women show more passive hip flexion (about 6°) and internal rotation (about 8°) than men. These are averages, with large overlap between people.
4. *Hip range and low back pain.* [S] Of the hip movements measured, only internal rotation was lower in people with non-specific low back pain, and the evidence is very low quality. The claim is cross-sectional: it shows an association, not a cause.
5. *How to see it.* [P] Watch a squat and a hip hinge from the side and from the front. Describe where the pelvis and lower back move, in plain words.
6. *What to do.* [P] Compare a client with themselves over time, not with a chart.
7. [!] Stop rules beside the observation drill: pain that comes on or rises, pins and needles, or a new inability to move the leg (hip §3).

## 3. Mobility, range and hip shape

**One idea:** hip shape differs between people, and many people with "abnormal" imaging have no pain. A trainer works with the range a client has.

**Objectives**
- Explain that bony hip shape findings are common in people without hip pain.
- Use the expert terminology: "morphology", not "deformity".
- Say what resistance training does for range of motion.
- Decide when a restriction is a training question and when it needs referral.

**Sections**
1. *Hip shape varies.* [S] Cam morphology is a bony prominence where the head of the thigh bone meets its neck. Imaging studies of people without hip pain found it in about a third of hips: 37% overall, 54.8% of athletes and 23.1% of the general population. The block may quote these figures, framed as imaging findings. It does not quote the pooled pincer figure, which the review itself calls poorly defined.
2. *More common in athletes.* [S] Cam morphology is more common in athletes than in non-athletes without symptoms.
3. *Words matter.* [S] An expert consensus recommends "morphology" over "deformity" or "abnormality", and describes primary cam morphology as a mostly benign bony prominence. [P] So a client told they have an "abnormal hip" is not told anything new by the trainer: the trainer never interprets imaging.
4. *Range improves with strength training.* [S] Across trials in healthy participants, resistance training improved range of motion. The working statement says only that range improved; anything more waits for the full text.
5. *Squat depth and stance.* **GAP.** We found no systematic review linking hip shape to squat depth or stance width. Coaching language about individual stance stays practice-based ([P] "find the stance and depth this client controls"). It makes no claim that bone shape is the reason.
6. *How to see it.* [P] Hip rotation lying on the back and lying face down, and a supported deep squat. Compare sides, and compare the client's active range with the range you can move them through (assisted, with permission).
7. *What to do.* [P] For a long-standing, familiar, painless restriction with no warning sign: train in the range the client controls, change one thing at a time, and re-check. Anything new, unexplained or painful goes to hip §3 and §4 first.
8. [!] Pain in the groin or hip during deep flexion or rotation means no further hip loading this session (hip §3, Spindle policy as SAFETY §3). Stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes after waking, or pain that disturbs sleep, is a routine referral (hip §4).

## 4. Assessments, screens and red flags

**One idea:** screens show a trainer where to look and when to stop. They never diagnose and never clear anyone.

**Objectives**
- Run three simple screens with clear start positions and what to look for.
- Explain why clinical hip tests have limited accuracy, even in clinicians' hands.
- Ask the questions that surface red flags before training the hip.
- Choose the right referral tier.

**Sections**
1. *Why screens are not tests.* [S] Clinical tests for FAI and labral tears have limited diagnostic accuracy, and few of the studies were high quality. An expert consensus on hip-related pain in young and middle-aged active adults adds two points. Within a comprehensive clinical assessment, a negative flexion-adduction-internal rotation test may help clinicians rule hip-related pain out. Examination and imaging have limited diagnostic use on their own. The block says these are clinicians' tests. Trainers do not use them to diagnose.
2. *Range screening does not predict.* [S] In athletes, lower total hip rotation at pre-season screening was associated with groin pain, but the authors say screening is unlikely to identify who will develop it.
3. *Before you screen: questions.* [P] Recent fall or injury? Pins and needles or numbness? Night pain? Fever or feeling unwell? Any diagnosed hip, back or pelvic condition, or recent surgery? Pregnant or recently given birth? Each question maps to hip §2 to §4.
4. [!] Red flags in trainer words, with tiers (hip §4 in full). The consensus names serious conditions that clinicians must exclude, which supports the referral rule. The trainer's job is to recognize warning signs, never to name a condition.
5. *Screen 1: supported squat.* [P] Start position, what to look for, how to compare sides and reps. [!] Stop rules beside it.
6. *Screen 2: single-leg stand and step-down.* [P] Start position and what to look for: the pelvis, the knee path, and control. [!] Stop rules beside it.
7. *Screen 3: hip rotation range.* [P] Lying on the back or seated, compared by side. [!] Stop rules beside it.
8. *What to do.* [P] Record what you saw in plain words. Choose: train, adjust, or refer, and which tier. Re-check after a change.

**Scenario (ungraded), adverse cases:** these stay ungraded until claims are approved.
- Severe hip pain after a fall in class; the client cannot put weight on the leg (emergency).
- Back pain with numbness around the genitals or buttocks, or new bladder or bowel changes (emergency).
- A hip that is suddenly very painful and hot, with the client feeling unwell (emergency under hip §4, Spindle policy).
- A mild ache in the front of the hip after deep squats that settles within a day, with no pain during the sets and no warning signs (continue; consider range or load next session).
- Hip pain not better after 2 weeks of avoiding the provoking movement (routine).

## 5. Glute activation and warm-ups: what the evidence supports

**One idea:** exercise choice changes which hip muscles work hardest. "Activation" drills are warm-up and rehearsal, not a fix.

**Objectives**
- Explain that different exercises emphasize different hip muscles, and what that evidence can and cannot tell you.
- Separate what research measured from coaching practice (activation sequences), without ranking approaches.
- Build a short, ordered warm-up for the hips.

**Sections**
1. *Exercises emphasize different muscles.* [S] A systematic review of muscle-activity studies (predominantly using EMG) in healthy, active people found that lower-limb exercises activate individual muscles differently. Exercise choice therefore changes which muscle is emphasized. The block makes no claim that activation predicts strength, size or injury.
2. *Hip thrust before sprinting.* [S] A meta-analysis found that a hip thrust done as a warm-up conditioning activity gave a moderate, protocol-dependent short-term sprint benefit. The certainty is low to very low. The same review found that long-term hip-thrust training did not carry over to squat strength or jumping. This section is removed if no claim is approved.
3. *Glute "activation" drills.* **GAP.** We found no systematic review of glute activation drills for performance or injury prevention. The lesson says nothing about their effect. [P] What activation drills are for in a session: rehearsal, attention, warm-up. They are not a fix, and research does not rank them. (This is coaching practice, not a finding that they do not work.)
4. *How to see it.* [P] Does the client own the position under a light load? Re-check a lesson 4 screen.
5. *What to do.* [P] An ordered warm-up: position, then light hip movement in each direction, then single-leg control, then the session's first pattern with a light load. One to three cues each, and an easier version for each drill.
6. [!] Warm-ups never replace referral. Anything in hip §3 means stop. Pain that persists or worsens means routine referral.

## 6. Loading the hip: squat, hinge, lunge, hip thrust, carries

**One idea:** hips get stronger the way everything else does: progressive load through a range the client controls.

**Objectives**
- Choose squat, hinge, lunge, hip thrust and carry variations by range, control and goal.
- Progress load, range and complexity one at a time.
- Use and define cues for each pattern.
- Recognize the signs that mean "do not progress".

**Sections**
1. *What it is.* [S] The ACSM position stand recommends progressive resistance training for healthy adults to improve muscle function, size and physical performance. This reuses the shoulder module's `l6-progression` claim; it is not a new slot.
2. *Glute size.* [S] A meta-analysis found that resistance training moderately increased gluteus maximus size. The abstract says several exercises increased it, including deep back squats and the barbell hip thrust. The block lists options and ranks nothing.
3. *Range of motion.* [S] Full-range training gave greater strength and lower-limb hypertrophy gains than partial-range training. The block says "a full range the client controls without pain". It never pushes into pain.
4. *Dose.* [S] For muscle growth, the shoulder module's `l6-volume` and `l6-failure` claims apply unchanged. No new slot.
5. *Squat, hinge, lunge, hip thrust.* [P] Set-up, range, how to choose a variation, and cue definitions.
6. *Carries.* [P] Practice only. **GAP:** we found no review on carries, or one comparing lunges with hinges.
7. *Progressions.* [P] One variable at a time: range, then load, then complexity. Re-check after each change.
8. [!] Do not progress when pain rises with load, the client cannot control the range, or any hip §3 sign appears. This sits beside each progression.

## 7. Hip and groin pain in active adults

**One idea:** hip and groin pain has many sources. Trainers notice, describe, adjust and refer. Clinicians use agreed definitions to classify it.

**Objectives**
- Know that clinicians classify groin pain in athletes into agreed categories, and that trainers do not.
- Explain that FAI syndrome needs symptoms, clinical signs and imaging together, not a scan alone.
- Say what the evidence shows about adductor strength and groin-injury prevention, including its limits.
- Say what exercise research shows for hip osteoarthritis, and why clinical evidence is not a trainer's treatment plan.

**Sections**
1. *How clinicians name groin pain.* [S] An expert agreement classifies groin pain in athletes into three groups: defined clinical entities (adductor-, iliopsoas-, inguinal- and pubic-related), hip-related groin pain, and other causes. [P] This is clinicians' vocabulary, so the trainer describes and refers.
2. *What FAI syndrome means.* [S] An international consensus defines FAI syndrome as symptoms, clinical signs and imaging findings together. It names conservative care, rehabilitation and surgery as treatment options. Combined with lesson 3: a scan finding alone is not the syndrome.
3. *Findings without pain.* [S] In the same review of people without hip pain, labral injury appeared on MRI in about two-thirds of hips, in the studies that reported it.
4. *Adductor strength and groin pain.* [S] In healthy athletes, those who stayed uninjured had stronger hip adduction than those who later developed groin pain. Abduction strength differed trivially. This is an association from prospective cohorts, not proof that strengthening prevents groin pain.
5. *Prevention programs.* [S] Groin-injury prevention programs in sport showed a reduction in groin injuries that was not statistically significant. The trials were mainly in soccer and handball. The block reports the uncertainty.
6. *Hip osteoarthritis.* [S] Compared with no treatment, land-based exercise probably reduces hip osteoarthritis pain slightly (moderate certainty), and the difference may not be clinically meaningful. Compared with placebo or attention control, exercise may have little or no effect on pain (low certainty). **Spindle policy, not a science claim:** a trainer's loading is not treatment for a diagnosed condition. A client with diagnosed hip osteoarthritis trains within the treating clinician's plan (hip §2).
7. *What to do.* [P] Adjust range, load or variation in a later session, only if no warning sign remains. Keep training other areas while the hip is assessed, for a routine referral only.
8. [!] Pain while running or cutting that changes how the client moves, groin pain with a lump, or pain with fever or feeling unwell: stop and refer (hip §3, §4).

## 8. When to refer out

**One idea:** referral is a coaching skill. Notice, stop, refer to the right place at the right speed, and keep the client training what is safe.

**Objectives**
- Apply the hip referral tiers to scenarios.
- Use non-diagnostic language when referring.
- Know the excluded populations, including children and adolescents, and how to work alongside a clinician's plan.

**Sections**
1. *Scope.* [P] What a trainer does and does not do. A screen never clears anyone (SAFETY §1).
2. [!] *Emergency.* Criteria and actions (hip §4).
3. [!] *Urgent.* Criteria and actions (hip §4).
4. [!] *Routine.* Criteria and actions (hip §4).
5. *Excluded populations.* [!] Hip §2, with what to do instead.
6. *The conversation.* [P] Scripts by tier, as in the shoulder module.
7. *Working with a clinician's plan.* [P] Ask for the plan in writing. Do not progress it on your own.

**Graded quiz candidates:** graded adverse-case questions (SAFETY §6) need an approved claim for each criterion. The hip-related pain consensus (`hip-l4-hrp-diagnosis`) names conditions clinicians must exclude. That supports a referral question but not a tier, so tier questions stay ungraded. **GAP:** a hip care pathway equivalent to the BESS shoulder pathway (BESS covers the shoulder and elbow only). Candidates to look for next: an AAOS clinical practice guideline or a US physical therapy (APTA Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy) clinical practice guideline for hip pain.

**Scenario (ungraded), adverse cases:** one per tier plus two "continue" cases, drawn from hip §3 and §4. They include a 14-year-old with knee pain and a limp after training (urgent or emergency, hip §2) and severe hip pain after a fall (emergency).

## Safety before drafting: hip rows

SAFETY.md covers the shoulder. Before any hip lesson is drafted, these rows move into a hip safety document (or a hip section of SAFETY.md). The document must follow the same rules: every criterion cites a source, our tier mapping is labeled as our own, and anything without a source says **Spindle policy**.

Care pathways are US pathways (D-18), as in SAFETY.md: **emergency** means call 911 now, and never drive the client or suggest they get a ride; **urgent** means urgent care or the client's primary care provider (PCP) the same day; **routine** means a primary care provider or a licensed physical therapist (direct access to physical therapy varies by state). The rows were first written against UK NHS pages; those pages are retired as sources under D-18. The criteria and tiers are unchanged, except one stricter emergency row for a stuck hernia (marked below). Where no US source read so far supports a criterion, it is labeled **Spindle policy** and listed under the known gaps.

**Hip §4. Referral tiers.** These use the same structure as SAFETY §4: use the highest tier any sign matches.
- **Emergency (call 911 now; never private transport):**
  - cannot walk or put weight on the leg;
  - after a fall or injury: severe hip pain, a leg that looks shorter or at an odd angle, or tingling or loss of feeling in the hip or leg;
  - back pain with the signs in hip §3 (both legs; around the genitals or anus; bladder or bowel; sexual sensation or function);
  - suspected joint infection (Spindle policy, hip §3);
  - possible DVT with breathlessness or chest pain;
  - the SAFETY §4 cardiac criterion, unchanged: chest pain that feels tight, crushing or squeezing, or that spreads to the arm, neck or jaw; severe difficulty breathing; collapse or not responding normally (CDC heart attack; MedlinePlus EMS);
  - a groin lump or hernia with any sign NIDDK lists for a stuck or strangulated hernia: a bulge that is suddenly larger, or that used to go back in and no longer does; fever; redness at the bulge; sudden or severe pain or tenderness at the bulge; or belly pain, bloating, nausea or vomiting. NIDDK calls a strangulated hernia life-threatening and says to seek medical help right away. **New under D-18 and stricter than the earlier urgent row; LEAD to confirm.**
  - a child's emergency signs (hip §2).
- **Urgent (urgent care or the client's primary care provider, the same day):**
  - severe hip pain that started suddenly, without a fall or injury (Spindle policy);
  - swelling around the hip, difficulty moving the hip, a hip that is swollen and hot, or skin around it that has changed color: each on its own (hip §3);
  - possible DVT: pain or tenderness, swelling, warmth, red or discolored skin, or swollen veins, usually in one leg (hip §3);
  - any other child or adolescent with hip, thigh or knee pain or a limp;
  - a groin lump or hernia with any of: pain in or around it, constipation, feeling hot, cold or shivery, or sudden confusion, when no emergency sign above applies (Spindle policy, kept from the earlier mapping; vomiting, nausea, bloating, fever and severe pain now sit in the emergency row).
- **Routine (a primary care provider or a licensed physical therapist):**
  - hip pain that is getting worse, keeps coming back, has not improved after 2 weeks, stops normal activities, or affects sleep (Spindle policy, matching SAFETY §4);
  - stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes after waking (Spindle policy);
  - a groin lump or suspected hernia with none of the urgent or emergency signs above (NIDDK inguinal hernia: a bulge in the groin is the usual symptom, and a doctor assesses it);
  - persistent or unusual pain or swelling in or near a bone (NCI bone cancer: such pain or swelling has many causes, cancer among them, and a doctor assesses it); and bone pain or tenderness that is worse at night (**Spindle policy**: NCI does not name night pain). The routine tier for both is **Spindle policy**. This is a routine primary care visit, never framed as cancer to the client.

**Known gaps for the hip safety document**
- No US public-domain source read yet for: the child and adolescent limp rows, septic arthritis, the cauda equina emergency tier, the hip pain routine thresholds (2 weeks, keeps coming back, stops normal activities, affects sleep), morning stiffness, and night pain. Each is labeled Spindle policy. Candidates: AAOS OrthoInfo pages on limping in children, transient synovitis and septic arthritis (rights reserved; paraphrase only); NIAMS and NICHD pages; the American Academy of Pediatrics HealthyChildren.org pages.
- No US page for Perthes disease or slipped capital femoral epiphysis read yet.
- No US page for stress fractures read yet. The hip-related pain consensus lists stress fracture among the serious conditions clinicians must exclude, but gives no tier.
- No specialist hip care pathway read yet: the hip has no counterpart to BESS.
- Night pain: hip pain that affects sleep is a routine referral as Spindle policy. Bone pain that is worse at night is a separate, more specific routine criterion. Neither is a red flag on its own.
- The new emergency row for a stuck or strangulated hernia moves signs that were urgent in the earlier mapping (pain, a bloated belly, feeling or being sick, a high temperature) to emergency, because NIDDK calls a strangulated hernia life-threatening; the LEAD confirms it before the hip safety document is written.
