# Shoulder module: outline

Status: draft outline for review, 2026-10-11. Base layer. Eight lessons, written for working personal trainers. Text is ours (CC BY-SA 4.0); style follows [STYLE.md](../../docs/curriculum/STYLE.md) and every lesson follows [SAFETY.md](../../docs/curriculum/SAFETY.md).

Nothing here is a lesson yet. Full drafting waits for the outline review and the claim and quiz contracts.

## How to read this outline

- **[S]** a statement that will be a *Science* block. It needs an approved claim before it can be published. "Candidate evidence" names where we expect to look; nothing is cited until the claim pipeline has verified the source.
- **[P]** a *Practice-based* block: coaching experience, cues, set-ups and progressions. It carries no claim and must not read like one.
- **No downgrades.** If a science candidate gets no approved claim, the statement is removed, or rewritten as a coaching recommendation that says nothing about how the body works or what research shows. It never keeps its content under a practice-based label.
- **[!]** a safety block, placed where the risk is. Wording follows SAFETY.md; "SAFETY §3" means its stop rules and "§4" its referral tiers.
- **Graded quiz** candidates are science-only and each binds a claim. **Scenario** candidates are ungraded discussion until the claims they need are approved.
- **Video slots** give a channel code and a topic only. Channel codes come from the private inspiration list; the LEAD admits each clip, the embed check runs, and the reviewer checks the segment. Videos are optional, practice-based, and never needed for completion.

Sections follow one pattern: *what it is*, *why it matters*, *how to see it*, *what to do*.

## 1. Shoulder anatomy a trainer needs

**One idea:** the shoulder is four joints working as one, and its big range comes from a shallow ball-and-socket that muscles keep centred.

**Objectives**
- Name the bones and joints of the shoulder complex in plain words and in standard terms.
- Explain why the glenohumeral joint trades stability for range.
- Describe what the rotator cuff does as a group.
- Point to where each structure is on a client, by surface landmarks.

**Sections**
1. *What it is: the parts.* [S] Bones: humerus, scapula, clavicle; joints: glenohumeral, acromioclavicular, sternoclavicular, and the scapulothoracic articulation (a gliding interface, not a true joint). Candidate evidence: official nomenclature (Terminologia Anatomica).
2. *What it is: a shallow socket.* [S] The glenoid is shallow relative to the humeral head; the labrum deepens the socket, and the capsule and its ligaments help hold the joint stable. Candidate evidence: anatomy reference with an open consensus or review source.
3. *What it is: the cuff.* [S] Four rotator cuff muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) and their role in keeping the humeral head centred during arm movement. Candidate evidence: a review of cuff function (e.g. a JOSPT clinical commentary on shoulder exercise rationale).
4. *What it is: the big movers.* [S] Deltoid, pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, trapezius, serratus anterior: what each moves. Candidate evidence: nomenclature plus a review.
5. *Why it matters.* [P] Range and control trade off; a trainer's job is to load range the client can control.
6. *How to see it.* [P] Palpation tour on yourself: clavicle, acromion, spine of the scapula, inferior angle. Coach note: say what you are touching and ask first.
7. *What to do.* [P] Use the plain names with clients and the standard names with clinicians; one-line glossary card.
8. [!] Short note: the lesson teaches location and function, never what is "wrong". A painful spot is described, not named as an injury (SAFETY §1).

**Visuals:** original diagram of the four joints (front and back views); diagram of the cuff around the humeral head.

**Graded quiz candidates**
- Which joint connects the arm to the scapula? (glenohumeral) [binds §1 claim]
- As a group, what does the rotator cuff do during arm movement? (helps keep the humeral head centred on the glenoid) [binds §3 claim]
- Which of these is not a true synovial joint? (scapulothoracic) [binds §1 claim]

**Scenario (ungraded):** A client points to the front of the shoulder and asks "Is that my rotator cuff?" What do you say? (Describe the area; do not diagnose; if it hurts, follow SAFETY.)

**Video slots:** C2: anatomy overview of the shoulder; C1: one cuff muscle explained.

## 2. Scapulohumeral rhythm

**One idea:** when the arm goes up, the shoulder blade moves with it; watching that shared movement is a core trainer skill.

**Objectives**
- Describe how the scapula moves when the arm is raised: upward rotation and posterior tilt, with internal or external rotation that depends on the plane and range.
- Explain that the humerus and scapula share elevation, and that the ratio varies.
- Recognise the muscle pairs that rotate the scapula upward.
- Watch a client's arm raise and describe what you see in plain words.

**Sections**
1. *What it is.* [S] During arm elevation the scapula upwardly rotates and tilts posteriorly. Its internal or external rotation varies with the plane of elevation and through the range, so it is not taught as one fixed pattern. Candidate evidence: review of scapular kinematics (JOSPT 2009, open in PMC) and an in vivo 3D kinematic study.
2. *What it is: the shared ratio.* [S] Arm elevation is shared between glenohumeral and scapulothoracic motion; the often-quoted ratio is about 2:1 overall, and it varies by person, plane and load. Candidate evidence: classic kinematic study plus a modern review. The block must say the ratio varies.
3. *What it is: the force couple.* [S] Upper and lower trapezius with serratus anterior rotate the scapula upward. Candidate evidence: review of exercise rationale for scapular muscles.
4. *Why it matters.* [S] Altered scapular movement is common in people with and without shoulder pain, so on its own it is not a diagnosis. Candidate evidence: scapular dyskinesis consensus statement (2013).
5. *How to see it.* [P] Watch from behind: raise and lower the arms slowly, 3 to 5 reps; look at symmetry, timing and the inferior angle on the way down. Light load can make it clearer.
6. *What to do.* [P] Cues for overhead work: "reach long through the hands", "let the shoulder blades travel". Define "shoulder blades back and down" and when it does not fit overhead (STYLE › Cues).
7. [!] Stop rules beside the observation drill: pain that comes on or rises during the raise, tingling, or an arm that cannot be lifted (SAFETY §3).

**Visuals:** original animation-free diagram of scapular upward rotation at three arm angles.

**Graded quiz candidates**
- Which two scapular motions happen consistently as the arm is raised overhead? (Upward rotation and posterior tilt; internal/external rotation varies with plane and range) [§1]
- True or false: the glenohumeral-to-scapular ratio is a fixed 2:1 in everyone. (False: it varies) [§2]
- Which muscles form the upward rotation force couple? [§3]
- Does a visible change in scapular movement alone mean the client has a shoulder injury? (No) [§4]

**Scenario (ungraded):** As the client lowers the arms from overhead, the inner edge of one shoulder blade lifts slightly away from the ribs compared with the other side; the client has no pain. Continue, adjust, or refer? (Continue with load they control; observe; no referral without warning signs.)

**Video slots:** C5: scapular function in pressing; C2: watching the arm raise from behind.

## 3. Mobility and stability: common restrictions

**One idea:** a missing range is a question to answer with a careful look, not a label; some restrictions are normal, some need a clinician.

**Objectives**
- Distinguish active from passive range, and range from control.
- Recognise common restrictions trainers see: overhead reach, internal rotation, horizontal reach across the body.
- Explain what research says about posture and shoulder pain.
- Decide when a restriction is a training question and when it needs referral.

**Sections**
1. *What it is.* [P] Mobility (range you can reach) and stability (control of that range) as two coaching questions, not opposites.
2. *Active vs passive.* [P] Range you can reach yourself versus range someone else can move you through. A difference is a reason to look closer, not a prescription; this lesson draws no training conclusion from it. [!] A new or substantial loss of active movement that has no clear explanation (the client cannot lift the arm themselves, even if you can move it for them) is an urgent referral, never something to train (SAFETY §3, §4).
3. *Posture.* [S] A systematic review found moderate evidence of no significant difference in thoracic kyphosis (how rounded the upper back is) between people with and without shoulder pain. A non-significant result does not prove the groups are the same, the included studies varied in quality, and one high-risk-of-bias study found more kyphosis with pain. The claim is limited to thoracic posture and to association; it says nothing about rounded shoulders or about cause. Candidate evidence: systematic review of thoracic posture and shoulder pain, range and function (2016). The block must not describe posture as damage.
4. *Upright posture and overhead reach.* [S] In single-session studies, people reach more maximum shoulder range in an upright posture than in a slouched one, with or without shoulder pain; whether that matters over time is unclear. Candidate evidence: the same 2016 systematic review. If no claim is approved, the section is removed (no downgrade).
5. *How to see it.* [P] Overhead reach against a wall, standing back-to-wall arm raise, and a supine reach: compare sides and compare active with passive.
6. *What to do.* [P] For a long-standing, familiar, painless restriction with no warning sign: train in the range the client can control, change one thing at a time, and re-check. Anything new, unexplained or painful goes to SAFETY §3 and §4 first.
7. [!] Stiffness that does not go away, a big loss of movement, or pain that is worse at night: do not keep stretching into it; routine referral (SAFETY §3, §4). After an injury, an arm the client cannot move: emergency. A new loss of active movement without an injury: urgent.
8. [P] Contested models (for example fascial lines or rib-position stories) as clearly labelled practice context with a limited-evidence note, if used at all.

**Graded quiz candidates**
- What did a systematic review find when it compared upper-back rounding (thoracic kyphosis) in people with and without shoulder pain? (No significant difference, with moderate evidence; that does not prove the groups are the same, and it does not show cause) [§3]

**Scenario (ungraded):** A client cannot reach overhead on either side; with permission you can lift the arms higher, and nothing hurts. What do you do first? (First ask about onset and check the warning signs: is it new, did anything happen, is there pain, numbness, weakness, night pain or worsening? Anything new, unexplained or painful goes to SAFETY §3 and §4. Only for a familiar, long-standing, painless restriction with no warning sign: train in the range the client controls, change one thing, and re-check in a later session. The gap between active and passive range does not decide the plan.) Variant: stiffness for months with pain at night, getting worse (routine referral now, because it is getting worse).

**Video slots:** C2: shoulder stability progression from easy to hard; C3: mobility and strength framing (practice context only).

## 4. Assessments, screens and red flags

**One idea:** screens tell a trainer where to look and when to stop; they never diagnose and never clear anyone.

**Objectives**
- Run three simple screens with clear start positions and what to look for.
- Explain why a trainer's screen can neither diagnose nor clear a shoulder.
- Ask the questions that surface red flags before training the shoulder.
- Choose the right referral tier from what you see and hear.

**Sections**
1. *Why screens are not tests.* [S] Even in clinicians' hands, individual physical examination tests have limited accuracy for identifying specific shoulder conditions; some help clinicians rule a condition in or out when combined with other findings. Candidate evidence: recent systematic review and meta-analysis of shoulder examination for subacromial bursitis and rotator cuff tears (2024, BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders, doi:10.1186/s12891-024-08144-z, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0); the 2012 meta-analysis update only as a bounded historical example.
2. *Before you screen: questions.* [P] Recent injury? Pins and needles or numbness? Night pain? Fever or feeling unwell? Any diagnosed shoulder or neck condition? (Each maps to SAFETY §2 to §4.)
3. [!] Red flags list in trainer words, with tiers (SAFETY §4 in full).
4. *Screen 1: arm raise from behind.* [P] Start position, what to look for, what a side-to-side difference suggests. [!] Stop rules beside it.
5. *Screen 2: rotation range.* [P] Supine or standing external and internal rotation, compared by side. [!] Stop rules beside it.
6. *Screen 3: reach patterns.* [P] Reach behind the head and behind the back, compared by side. [!] Stop rules beside it.
7. *What to do.* [P] Record what you saw in plain words; choose: train, adjust, or refer (and which tier). Re-check after a change.
8. [P] Scripts for the referral conversation that do not alarm or diagnose.

**Graded quiz candidates**
- Can a trainer use one screen to diagnose a shoulder problem or to clear a shoulder for training? (No) [§1, with SAFETY §1]
- Adverse case questions bind a claim only when one is approved for that criterion; otherwise they stay in the scenario set.

**Scenario (ungraded), adverse cases**
- Shoulder looks out of place after a fall in class (emergency).
- Pain and weakness lifting the arm after catching a heavy bar; the arm still moves and looks normal (urgent). Variant: after the same event the client cannot move the arm (emergency).
- Pins and needles in the arm or hand that do not go away (urgent).
- A mild ache after a pressing session that settles within a day, with no pain during the sets and no warning signs (continue; consider load or range for the next session; stop if pain comes on during a set).
- Shoulder pain not better after 2 weeks of avoiding the provoking movement (routine).

**Video slots:** C2: overhead mobility screen; none for red flags (text only).

## 5. Correctives and activation: what the evidence supports

**One idea:** "activation" drills are useful warm-ups and practice for control. Research on exercise for shoulder pain is clinical research, and it does not rank one approach above the others for trainers.

**Objectives**
- Explain what exercise therapy research shows for subacromial shoulder pain, and why that clinical evidence is not a trainer's treatment plan.
- Separate what the research compared from what is coaching practice (activation sequences), without ranking approaches.
- Build a short, ordered warm-up for the shoulder.
- Know when a "corrective" should be a referral instead.

**Sections**
1. *What research supports.* [S] For subacromial shoulder pain (one specific condition, diagnosed by a clinician), UK specialist guidance reports evidence that exercise is effective at reducing pain in the short term (8 weeks to 6 months) and improving patient-reported function, and states that the most effective physiotherapy or exercise approach remains uncertain. A large trial found that surgery to decompress the space gave no extra benefit over an arthroscopy without decompression. Candidate evidence: BESS care pathway (2025); CSAW randomised trial (2018, PMID 29169668). Wording stays within the population studied, and the claim says nothing about who delivers the exercise (BESS also describes initial self-management). Separately, as **Spindle policy** and not as a science claim: a trainer's loading is not treatment for a diagnosed condition, and a client with diagnosed subacromial pain trains within the treating clinician's plan (SAFETY §2).
2. *What the trials compared.* [S] For rotator cuff related shoulder pain, a 2020 systematic review compared each approach with placebo or no treatment, not with each other: progressive resisted exercise showed an uncertain clinical benefit, non-progressive exercise no significant benefit, and the certainty of the evidence was low. The block reports those comparisons and that uncertainty and ranks nothing. Candidate evidence: Naunton et al. 2020, Clinical Rehabilitation (PMID 32571081). If no claim is approved, the section is removed (no downgrade).
3. *Scapular exercise.* [S] Candidate claim on exercises with high serratus anterior and lower trapezius activity relative to upper trapezius. Candidate evidence: EMG-based reviews of scapular exercise. Keep EMG claims modest: activity is not strength gain, and no exercise is called best.
4. *Activation as practice.* [P] What activation drills are for in a session: rehearsal, attention, warm-up. What they are not: a fix.
5. *How to see it.* [P] Does the client own the position under a light load? Re-check the screen from lesson 4.
6. *What to do.* [P] Ordered warm-up: position, then light rotation work, then scapular control, then the session's first pattern with a light load. One to three cues each. Easier version for each drill.
7. [!] "Correctives" never replace referral: anything on SAFETY §3 means stop; persistent or worsening pain means routine referral.

**Graded quiz candidates**
- For subacromial shoulder pain diagnosed by a clinician, what does UK specialist guidance report about exercise? (It reduces pain in the short term and improves function; the most effective approach remains uncertain) [§1]
- Does higher EMG activity in an exercise mean it builds more strength? (Not necessarily) [§3]

**Scenario (ungraded):** A client wants to "activate" for 20 minutes before every session. What do you keep, and what do you move into the main session?

**Video slots:** C2: three everyday shoulder drills with easy versions; C1: warm-up for the shoulders.

## 6. Loading the shoulder: pressing, pulling, overhead, progressions

**One idea:** shoulders get stronger the way everything else does: progressive load through a range the client controls, with balanced pressing and pulling.

**Objectives**
- Choose pressing, pulling and overhead variations by range, control and goal.
- Progress load, range and complexity one at a time.
- Use cues for the press, the row and the overhead press, and say what each means.
- Recognise the signs that mean "do not progress".

**Sections**
1. *What it is.* [S] Resistance training increases muscle strength and size across the shoulder muscles when load is progressive. Candidate evidence: position stand or review on resistance training progression.
2. *Pressing.* [P] Bench, push-up, landmine: set-up, range, and choosing a variation. Cue definitions (for example "push the floor away", "bend the bar").
3. *Pulling.* [P] Rows and pulldowns: range and scapular movement; balance pulling volume with pressing.
4. *Overhead.* [P] Landmine to half-kneeling to standing press; when to pick which. [S] Candidate claim: overhead pressing requires full arm elevation with upward scapular rotation (links back to lesson 2).
5. *Hypertrophy angle.* [S] Candidate claims on training volume and proximity to failure for muscle growth, kept general. Candidate evidence: meta-analyses on volume. If no claim is approved, the section is removed (no downgrade).
6. *Progressions.* [P] One variable at a time: range, then load, then complexity. Re-check after each change.
7. [!] Do not progress when pain rises with load, the client cannot control the range, or any SAFETY §3 sign appears. Beside each progression.

**Graded quiz candidates**
- What drives strength gains over time? (Progressive overload) [§1]
- Which scapular motion must happen for full overhead reach? (Upward rotation) [§4]

**Scenario (ungraded):** A client's shoulder starts to ache at the bottom of a deep dumbbell bench press during the set; push-ups earlier felt fine. What do you do now, and what might change next time? (Now: stop shoulder loading for this session, per SAFETY §3. Next session, only if no warning sign remains: discuss a shorter range or another variation, and stop again if pain comes on. Refer routinely if the pain is getting worse, or has not improved after 2 weeks.)

**Video slots:** C6: overhead press technique; C4: exercise selection for the deltoids; C7: evidence on shoulder exercise selection (a segment that explains choices, never a ranking).

## 7. Throwing and rotation

**One idea:** throwing puts the shoulder through fast, large rotations; trainers support throwers with total rotation, strength and sensible workload, not by "fixing" the adaptations throwers develop.

**Objectives**
- Describe the phases of a throw and where shoulder loads peak.
- Explain throwers' typical range adaptations (more external rotation, less internal rotation) and total rotation.
- Choose strength and rotation work for overhead and rotational athletes.
- Know the warning signs in throwers and when to refer.

**Sections**
1. *What it is.* [S] Phases of the throw (wind-up, cocking, acceleration, deceleration, follow-through) and the large forces at the shoulder during arm cocking and deceleration. Candidate evidence: biomechanics study of pitching kinetics (1995).
2. *Adaptations and injury risk.* [S] Throwers often show more external and less internal rotation in the throwing arm. Whether range predicts injury is uncertain and depends on the sport. A 2018 meta-analysis of overhead athletes found that no range measure reached statistical significance for predicting shoulder or elbow injury, though results leaned toward more injury with internal rotation loss. A 2020 meta-analysis of prospective cohorts found that too little extra external rotation in the throwing arm was associated with injury in professional baseball pitchers (pooled from three studies), suggested that range screening may not identify risk in handball, softball, volleyball or tennis players, and asked for caution because studies were few and varied. A 2025 systematic review across overhead sports listed range of motion among several risk factors, alongside reduced strength, previous pain or injury and training load, and noted that studies define injury inconsistently. The block presents this as current uncertainty, not as a screening rule. Candidate evidence: Pozzi et al. 2020, Br J Sports Med (systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohorts, PMID 31937577, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2019-100698, CC BY); Salamh et al. 2025, Int J Sports Phys Ther 20(3) (systematic review, doi:10.26603/001c.129462, CC BY-NC 4.0); Keller et al. 2018, Sports Health (PMID 29381423); Wilk et al. 2011 (a case series in professional pitchers, level of evidence 4, PMID 21131681) only as a bounded historical example.
3. *Why it matters.* [P] Do not "correct" a normal thrower's adaptation; compare total rotation between sides and over time.
4. *How to see it.* [P] Measure rotation the same way each time; note change across a season.
5. *What to do.* [P] Strength for the cuff and scapular muscles, trunk and hip rotation work, deceleration work; workload is the coach's and clinician's decision.
6. [P] Rotational sports beyond throwing (racquet, swimming, combat) as practice context.
7. [!] Pain while throwing, loss of velocity with pain, a "dead arm", or numbness: stop throwing and refer (SAFETY §3, §4). Young throwers with shoulder pain: urgent tier, an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 the same day for referral to trauma and orthopaedics, with a parent or carer told (SAFETY §2, §4).

**Graded quiz candidates**
- In which phases are shoulder forces highest in a throw? (Arm cocking and deceleration) [§1]
- What range pattern is common in a thrower's throwing arm? (More external, less internal rotation) [§2]

**Scenario (ungraded):** A 15-year-old pitcher says the shoulder hurts after games. What do you do? (Stop throwing and shoulder loading in your session; urgent tier: tell the athlete and a parent or carer to get an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 the same day, for referral to trauma and orthopaedics; emergency if any emergency sign is present. SAFETY §2, §4.)

**Video slots:** C1: when to train internal rotation for throwers.

## 8. When to refer out

**One idea:** referral is a coaching skill: notice, stop, refer to the right place at the right speed, and keep the client training what is safe.

**Objectives**
- Apply the three referral tiers from SAFETY.md to scenarios.
- Use non-diagnostic language when referring.
- Know the excluded populations and how to work alongside a clinician's plan.
- Keep training safe parts of the body while the shoulder is assessed.

**Sections**
1. *Scope.* [P] What a trainer does and does not do; a screen never clears anyone (SAFETY §1).
2. [!] *Emergency.* Criteria and actions (SAFETY §4).
3. [!] *Urgent.* Criteria and actions (SAFETY §4).
4. [!] *Routine.* Criteria and actions (SAFETY §4).
5. [S] For subacromial shoulder pain, BESS guidance reports that most symptoms settle within about three months with initial care, which includes self-management and exercise. Candidate evidence: BESS care pathway (2025). The claim stays within that population; it keeps routine referral calm and is never used to delay an urgent or emergency referral.
6. *Excluded populations.* [!] SAFETY §2, with what to do instead.
7. *The conversation.* [P] Scripts: what to say, what not to say, and how to keep the client moving with the rest of the session.
8. *Working with a clinician's plan.* [P] Ask for the plan in writing; do not progress it on your own.

**Graded quiz candidates**
- For subacromial shoulder pain, what does BESS guidance say about how most people do over about three months? (Most symptoms settle within about three months with initial care) [§5]

**Scenario (ungraded), adverse cases:** one per tier plus two "continue" cases, drawn from SAFETY §3 and §4, including arm pain with chest tightness (emergency) and a lump around the shoulder (urgent).

**Video slots:** C2: shoulder pain myths and when to see someone; C4: a critique of fear-based shoulder messaging (check referral handling).
