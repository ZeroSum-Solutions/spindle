# Shoulder safety rules

Status: draft for review, 2026-10-11; converted to US care pathways under D-18 the same day. These rules bind every base shoulder lesson, its quiz and any video segment we place in it. The reviewer applies them to every lesson review. They are not clinical guidance, and Spindle is not a clinical service.

Every criterion below cites a source from the list at the end, by its id (for example [S1]). The three referral tiers are **our own mapping** of those sources onto what a trainer in the United States can act on; no single source defines them. Where a rule has no direct source, it says **Spindle policy** and names the nearest support. We paraphrase every source. Short quotes are marked and attributed.

Care pathways are US pathways (D-18): 911 and the emergency department, urgent care or the client's primary care provider the same day, and a primary care provider or a licensed physical therapist for routine follow-up. US public-domain sources come first for that wording. The clinical red flags still rest on specialist shoulder guidance from the British Elbow & Shoulder Society (BESS) where no US source states them: clinical facts are international, and only the care pathway is American.

## 1. Trainer scope and wording

A personal trainer does not diagnose or treat injuries. A trainer notices, stops, and refers [S11]. US trainer guidance calls it best practice to refer a client with a suspected injury to a physician, who can refer on to an orthopedic specialist, a physical therapist or an athletic trainer [S11].

Rules for lesson text, quizzes and video context:

- **Nothing is diagnostic.** A lesson never names a condition as the cause of what a trainer sees. It describes what the trainer observes and what to do next [S11].
- **A screen never clears anyone.** A screen shows where to look and how a movement looks today. It does not rule out injury, and it is never a reason to load through pain or warning signs (Spindle policy; supported by [S11] and the referral rules in [S1], [S2]).
- **Name the observation, not the diagnosis.**

  | Write | Never write |
  |---|---|
  | "The arm stops short of overhead on the left side." | "They have impingement." |
  | "Pain came on during the press and changed with load. Stop and refer." | "It's just a tight pec; stretch it out." |
  | "This screen is one way to see how the shoulder blade moves." | "This test clears the shoulder for overhead work." |
  | "A physical therapist or doctor can assess this." | "This will fix the rotator cuff." |

- **Promise nothing about outcomes.** No "fixes", "bulletproofs" or "cures".
- **Refer to the right place in plain words:** call 911 or go to the emergency department (ED, often called the ER); urgent care or the client's primary care provider (PCP) the same day; or a primary care provider or a licensed physical therapist. If the problem may be life-threatening, call 911 [S14], [S15].
- **Physical therapy without a referral.** Every US state allows some form of direct access to a physical therapist, but the provisions and limits differ by state [S21]. A trainer may suggest seeing a licensed physical therapist; the trainer never implies a diagnosis, and the physical therapist decides whether the client also needs a physician.
- **Keep routine referrals calm.** US orthopedic patient guidance says that when shoulder pain is less severe it may be safe to rest for a few days, and to see a doctor if symptoms persist [S20]. For subacromial shoulder pain, BESS reports that most people improve within about three months [S3]. That figure is for subacromial pain only; it is never used to delay an urgent or emergency referral.

## 2. Excluded populations

The base shoulder module teaches trainers to work with healthy adults whose shoulders feel fine or have mild, familiar, load-tolerant aches. It does not teach a trainer to program for the groups below. A lesson that mentions them says "refer, or work under the plan of the treating clinician".

| Population | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Recent injury or accident, such as a fall, with new shoulder pain that has not been assessed | Do not train the shoulder. Use the referral tiers in section 4. | US federal guidance sends minor injuries such as strains, sprains and minor broken bones to urgent care, and emergencies to 911 or the emergency room [S15]; a shoulder dislocation is an emergency injury [S18]. BESS pathways treat an injury likely to cause a fracture or dislocation as a red flag [S1], [S3]. |
| After shoulder surgery, or a recent dislocation or fracture, still in rehabilitation | Outside the base module. Follow the treating clinician's plan; do not progress it on your own. | Spindle policy. Nearest support: get medical clearance and use the exercises the treating professional prescribes [S11]; after a dislocation, rehabilitation with physical therapy restores range and strength before load is added [S18]; the PAR-Q+ asks whether a doctor has said to do only medically supervised activity [S13]. |
| Diagnosed neurological condition affecting the arm or neck | Outside the base module. | Spindle policy. Nearest support: the PAR-Q+ follow-up asks about neurological conditions before exercise [S13]; numbness is among the listed symptoms of a dislocated shoulder [S18]. |
| Diagnosed shoulder condition under current care (for example frozen shoulder, a known cuff tear, instability) | Outside the base module; work only within the clinician's plan. | Physical therapy is the primary treatment for frozen shoulder [S19]. BESS lists cuff tears, instability and frozen shoulder among the conditions primary care must not miss [S3]. Over 40, a traumatic dislocation often comes with a cuff tear, which needs assessment [S8]. Training within the clinician's plan is Spindle policy. |
| Children and adolescents with shoulder pain | Outside the base module. **Urgent tier:** stop shoulder loading, and tell the young person and their parent or guardian to get care the same day, at urgent care or with their primary care provider, for assessment and, if needed, referral to orthopedics. Emergency tier if any emergency criterion applies. | BESS 2025 lists a child or adolescent with shoulder pain as a red flag that needs urgent specialist (orthopedic) assessment [S3]. The same-day US pathway is Spindle policy. |
| Anyone with a warning sign from section 3 or 4 | Stop and refer. | See sections 3 and 4. |

## 3. Stop rules

Stop the set and refer using section 4 when any of these appear. "Stop" always means: no further loading of that shoulder in this session. Changes to range, load or variation belong to a later session, and only if no warning sign remains.

| Stop when | What the trainer does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pain comes on or rises with load, or a movement seems to make the shoulder worse | Stop shoulder loading for this session. If the pain is getting worse, routine referral now: there is no need to wait. If it is not getting worse but has not improved after 2 weeks, routine referral. | US orthopedic guidance: rest a few days if the pain is less severe, and see a doctor if symptoms persist [S20]. The 2-week ceiling and the whole-session stop are **Spindle policy**; 2 weeks is a ceiling, never a reason to wait when pain is getting worse. |
| Numbness, pins and needles, or loss of feeling in the arm or shoulder | Stop. Pins and needles that do not go away, or no feeling, is urgent. | Spindle policy. Nearest support: numbness is a listed symptom of a dislocated shoulder [S18]; neurological symptoms belong to medical assessment before exercise [S13]. |
| After an injury, the client cannot move the arm, or the shoulder looks out of place or has changed shape | Stop. Do not move the arm or try to put it back. **Emergency.** | A shoulder dislocation is an emergency injury; deformity, swelling, numbness and weakness are among its symptoms, and alignment is restored in an emergency room [S18]. When symptoms cannot wait, call 911 rather than drive to an emergency room [S15]. BESS: an unreduced dislocation needs same-day emergency referral [S1], [S2], [S4]. |
| New loss of active movement: the client cannot lift or move the arm themselves (even if you could move it for them), without an injury | Stop. **Urgent.** Never treat this as a mobility or control problem to train. | BESS: a sudden loss of the ability to actively raise the arm, with or without trauma, needs urgent referral [S4]. |
| After an injury, pain and weakness, but the arm still moves and the shoulder looks normal | Stop. **Urgent** (possible acute cuff tear). | [S1], [S2], [S4]; urgent care treats strains, sprains and minor broken bones [S15]. |
| Signs of a joint infection: the shoulder is suddenly very painful **and** red or warm, or the client has a fever or feels unwell with shoulder pain | Stop. **Emergency.** | BESS: suspected joint infection needs same-day emergency referral [S1], [S2], [S4], immediate referral [S3]. See the note in section 4. |
| Arm pain together with chest pain, tightness, breathlessness, or feeling faint | Stop all exercise. **Emergency:** call 911. | Heart attack symptoms include chest pain or discomfort, pain or discomfort in one or both arms or shoulders, shortness of breath, and feeling weak, light-headed or faint; call 911 [S16], [S17]. |
| Pain at night that wakes the client, with stiffness that is not going away | Do not progress shoulder loading. **Routine.** | Spindle policy. Nearest support: frozen shoulder causes pain and stiffness, and the shoulder becomes very hard to move [S19]. We found no source that treats night pain alone as a red flag; we do not present it as one. |

## 4. Referral tiers

The trainer's job is to pick the tier and say it plainly. The trainer never decides what the problem is.

**Use the highest tier that any sign matches.** An emergency sign overrides every urgent or routine sign, and an urgent sign overrides every routine sign. Each presentation below appears in exactly one tier.

### Emergency: call 911 or go to the emergency department (ER) now

| Criterion | Source |
|---|---|
| Chest pain that feels tight, crushing or squeezing, or chest pain that spreads to the arm, neck or jaw; severe difficulty breathing; the person collapses or does not respond normally | [S16], [S17]; call 911 when a problem may be life-threatening [S14], [S15] |
| After an injury (a fall, an impact, a joint that "popped out", a seizure or an electric shock), the client cannot move the arm, or the shoulder looks out of place or has changed shape. Do not move the arm or try to put it back. Do not let the client drive themselves. | [S18], [S15]; BESS: an unreduced dislocation needs same-day emergency referral [S1], [S2], [S4] |
| Signs of a joint infection: the shoulder is suddenly very painful **and** red or warm, **or** the client has a fever or feels unwell with shoulder pain | BESS: a suspected infected joint needs same-day emergency referral [S1], [S2], [S4]; any evidence of infection needs immediate referral [S3] |

Note on infection: A trainer cannot tell whether a joint is infected, and BESS asks for same-day emergency referral when infection is suspected. Spindle's rule is therefore deliberately strict: fever or feeling unwell with shoulder pain goes to the emergency department, not to a same-day appointment. That strict mapping is **Spindle policy**. A shoulder that feels hot or cold to the touch, or is badly swollen, without sudden severe pain, redness, fever or feeling unwell, stays urgent.

### Urgent: urgent care or the client's primary care provider (PCP), the same day

Only when no emergency sign is present.

| Criterion | Source |
|---|---|
| Sudden or very bad shoulder pain | Spindle policy. Nearest support: only less severe pain is suited to a few days' rest [S20]; urgent care treats strains, sprains and minor broken bones [S15]. |
| New loss of active movement without an injury: the client cannot lift or move the arm themselves, even if it can be moved for them | BESS: sudden loss of the ability to actively raise the arm [S4] |
| After an injury, pain and weakness, while the arm still moves and the shoulder looks normal (possible acute cuff tear). BESS asks for urgent specialist assessment at the next available clinic; its 2014 guide gives under 2 weeks. | [S1], [S2], [S4]; [S15] |
| Any other new shoulder pain that started after an injury or accident, such as a fall | [S15]; Spindle policy for the same-day timing |
| The arm or shoulder has changed shape or is badly swollen, with no injury and no sign of infection | Spindle policy. Nearest support: deformity and swelling are listed symptoms of a dislocated shoulder [S18]. |
| Pins and needles that do not go away, or no feeling in the arm or shoulder | Spindle policy. Nearest support: [S18], [S13] |
| The arm or shoulder feels hot or cold to the touch, with no sign of infection (no sudden severe pain with redness or warmth, no fever, not unwell) | Spindle policy; no US source read yet (section 8). |
| Severe pain in both shoulders | Spindle policy. Nearest support: BESS treats inflammation in several joints as a red flag [S1], [S2], [S4]. |
| A new lump, mass or swelling around the shoulder | BESS: a suspected tumor is a red flag for fast-track specialist referral [S1], [S3], [S4] |
| New pain, swelling or stiffness in several joints at once | BESS: a rheumatological red flag that needs urgent referral [S1], [S2], [S4] |
| A child or adolescent with shoulder pain: urgent care or the PCP the same day, for assessment and, if needed, referral to orthopedics; tell a parent or guardian | BESS 2025: a red flag that needs urgent specialist (orthopedic) assessment [S3] |

### Routine: a primary care provider or a licensed physical therapist (direct access varies by state)

Only when no emergency or urgent sign is present.

| Criterion | Source |
|---|---|
| Shoulder pain that is getting worse. Refer now; do not wait for 2 weeks to pass. | [S20]; Spindle policy for "now" |
| Shoulder pain that is not getting worse but has not improved after 2 weeks | [S20]; the 2-week ceiling is Spindle policy |
| Moving the arm or shoulder is very difficult, but the client can still move it themselves | Spindle policy. Nearest support: [S19] |
| Pain and stiffness that do not go away, including pain that is worse at night | Spindle policy. Nearest support: [S19] |
| Pain that persists after the trainer has removed the provoking movements | [S20]; BESS pathways move to further assessment when symptoms do not respond to weeks of non-surgical care [S3], [S4] |

## 5. Where warnings sit

- **Beside each assessment and progression**, not only in the referral lesson. Every screen block is followed by a short "stop and refer if" block that links back to sections 3 and 4.
- Every loading progression names the signs that mean "do not progress".
- The referral lesson (lesson 8) teaches the tiers as a whole; earlier lessons repeat only the rules that apply at that point.
- Warning blocks are practice-based: they tell the trainer what to do and make no claim about the body. The sources here support the rules; they are not science claims in a lesson.

## 6. Quizzes

- The graded quiz includes adverse-case questions: given a short scenario, pick the correct action (continue, stop and refer routinely, urgent, or emergency).
- Every graded question binds an approved claim. Scenario questions that test the trainer's action against this document stay ungraded discussion until the claims they need are approved.
- Every scenario answer follows section 3: when pain appears, the answer stops shoulder loading for that session first.
- Wrong options never include a diagnostic action ("tell the client they have a cuff tear").

## 7. Review checklist

Each lesson review checks:

1. No diagnostic wording, and no screen that "clears" anyone.
2. Each assessment and progression has its stop and refer block.
3. No programming for an excluded population.
4. Every referral criterion in the lesson matches a row here, with the same tier.
5. Scenario answers stop loading for the session before suggesting any later change.
6. A missing or unapproved claim is never relabeled as practice: the statement is removed or rewritten as a coaching recommendation that says nothing about the body.
7. The chosen video segment and the context around it follow the same rules (no cure claims, no loading through warning signs).
8. Outcomes are not promised.
9. Care pathways are US pathways (911 and the emergency department; urgent care or the PCP the same day; a PCP or a licensed physical therapist), with no UK service named.

## 8. Known gaps

- **No US public-domain shoulder symptom page.** NIH's NIAMS shoulder page no longer resolves, and the MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia pages on shoulder pain, frozen shoulder and emergency room use are licensed from A.D.A.M. (Ebix), whose terms forbid automated access and any use with AI systems. Like OpenStax, they are not used as sources. Recheck NIAMS before launch.
- **Criteria kept as Spindle policy.** Under D-18 the UK NHS consumer pages that supported several urgent and routine criteria (sudden or very bad pain, swelling or a changed shape without injury, persistent pins and needles, a hot or cold arm, severe pain in both shoulders, the 2-week threshold, night pain with stiffness) were retired. The criteria and tiers are unchanged and are labeled Spindle policy with the nearest US support. Find a US source for each, or confirm them in the D-08 review by a US-licensed physical therapist.
- **Night pain** is not a red flag in any source we read. It is a routine referral.
- **Neurological deficit** appears in no BESS red-flag list we read, and no US source we read states a tier for it. Persistent pins and needles stays urgent as Spindle policy.
- **Post-surgical and neurological exclusions** are Spindle policy with the nearest support named.
- **Fever or feeling unwell as an emergency** is Spindle policy (section 4 note).
- **The 2-week cuff-tear figure** comes from the 2014 commissioning guide [S4]. The 2015, 2021 and 2025 pathways say "urgent" without a number.
- **AAOS OrthoInfo pages** [S18]–[S20] show a reviewer but no review date in the text we read. Recheck them before launch.
- **Outside the US:** the tiers name US services (D-18). A gym outside the US needs its own pathway wording before it uses these lessons.
