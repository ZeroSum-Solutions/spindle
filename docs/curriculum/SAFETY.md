# Shoulder safety rules

Status: draft for review, 2026-10-11. These rules bind every base shoulder lesson, its quiz and any video segment we place in it. The reviewer applies them to every lesson review. They are not clinical guidance, and Spindle is not a clinical service.

Every criterion below cites a source from the list at the end, by its id (for example [S1]). The three referral tiers are **our own mapping** of those sources onto what a trainer can act on; no single source defines them. Where a rule has no direct source, it says **Spindle policy** and names the nearest support. We paraphrase every source. Short quotes are marked and attributed.

## 1. Trainer scope and wording

A personal trainer does not diagnose or treat injuries. A trainer notices, stops, and refers [S11]. Knowing when to refer a client to a physiotherapist, doctor or other specialist is part of the role [S12].

Rules for lesson text, quizzes and video context:

- **Nothing is diagnostic.** A lesson never names a condition as the cause of what a trainer sees. It describes what the trainer observes and what to do next [S11].
- **A screen never clears anyone.** A screen shows where to look and how a movement looks today. It does not rule out injury, and it is never a reason to load through pain or warning signs (Spindle policy; supported by [S11] and the referral rules in [S1], [S2]).
- **Name the observation, not the diagnosis.**

  | Write | Never write |
  |---|---|
  | "The arm stops short of overhead on the left side." | "They have impingement." |
  | "Pain came on during the press and changed with load. Stop and refer." | "It's just a tight pec; stretch it out." |
  | "This screen is one way to see how the shoulder blade moves." | "This test clears the shoulder for overhead work." |
  | "A physiotherapist or doctor can assess this." | "This will fix the rotator cuff." |

- **Promise nothing about outcomes.** No "fixes", "bulletproofs" or "cures".
- **Refer to the right person in plain words:** emergency services, an urgent GP or NHS 111 appointment (or the local equivalent), or a physiotherapist or GP. Routine referral wording stays calm: NHS advice treats pain that is not improving as a reason to see a GP, not an emergency [S5], and for subacromial shoulder pain BESS reports that most people improve within about three months [S3]. That figure is for subacromial pain only; it is never used to delay an urgent or emergency referral.

## 2. Excluded populations

The base shoulder module teaches trainers to work with healthy adults whose shoulders feel fine or have mild, familiar, load-tolerant aches. It does not teach a trainer to programme for the groups below. A lesson that mentions them says "refer, or work under the plan of the treating clinician".

| Population | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Recent injury or accident, such as a fall, with new shoulder pain that has not been assessed | Do not train the shoulder. Use the referral tiers in section 4. | NHS lists pain that started after an injury or accident as an urgent reason to get help [S5]. BESS pathways treat an injury likely to cause a fracture or dislocation as a red flag [S1], [S3]. |
| After shoulder surgery, or a recent dislocation or fracture, still in rehabilitation | Outside the base module. Follow the treating clinician's plan; do not progress it on your own. | Spindle policy. Nearest support: get medical clearance and use the exercises the treating professional prescribes [S11]; after a dislocation, a doctor or physiotherapist advises on return to sport [S6]; the PAR-Q+ asks whether a doctor has said to do only medically supervised activity [S13]. |
| Diagnosed neurological condition affecting the arm or neck | Outside the base module. | Spindle policy. Nearest support: the PAR-Q+ follow-up asks about neurological conditions before exercise [S13]; persistent pins and needles or loss of feeling in the arm is an urgent reason to get help [S5]. |
| Diagnosed shoulder condition under current care (for example frozen shoulder, a known cuff tear, instability) | Outside the base module; work only within the clinician's plan. | NHS advises against strenuous exercise and gym equipment while the shoulder is painful [S5], [S7]. BESS lists cuff tears, instability and frozen shoulder among the conditions primary care must not miss [S3]. Over 40, a traumatic dislocation often comes with a cuff tear, which needs assessment [S8]. |
| Children and adolescents with shoulder pain | Outside the base module. **Urgent tier:** stop shoulder loading, and tell the young person and their parent or carer to get an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 (or the local equivalent) the same day, for referral to trauma and orthopaedics. Emergency tier if any emergency criterion applies. | BESS 2025 lists a child or adolescent with shoulder pain as a red flag for urgent secondary-care referral, to trauma and orthopaedics [S3]. |
| Anyone with a warning sign from section 3 or 4 | Stop and refer. | See sections 3 and 4. |

## 3. Stop rules

Stop the set and refer using section 4 when any of these appear. "Stop" always means: no further loading of that shoulder in this session. Changes to range, load or variation belong to a later session, and only if no warning sign remains.

| Stop when | What the trainer does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pain comes on or rises with load, or a movement seems to make the shoulder worse | Stop shoulder loading for this session. If the pain is getting worse, routine referral now: there is no need to wait. If it is not getting worse but has not improved after 2 weeks, routine referral. | NHS: do not do things that seem to make it worse; see a GP if pain is getting worse or does not improve after 2 weeks [S5], [S7]. |
| Numbness, pins and needles, or loss of feeling in the arm or shoulder | Stop. Pins and needles that do not go away, or no feeling, is urgent. | [S5] |
| After an injury, the client cannot move the arm, or the shoulder looks out of place or has changed shape | Stop. Do not move the arm or try to put it back. **Emergency.** | NHS dislocated shoulder: go to A&E if, after an injury, you cannot move your arm or the shoulder looks out of place [S6]; BESS: an unreduced dislocation needs same-day emergency referral [S1], [S2], [S4]. |
| New loss of active movement: the client cannot lift or move the arm themselves (even if you could move it for them), without an injury | Stop. **Urgent.** Never treat this as a mobility or control problem to train. | NHS: cannot move your arm is urgent [S5]; BESS: a sudden loss of the ability to actively raise the arm, with or without trauma, needs urgent referral [S4]. |
| After an injury, pain and weakness, but the arm still moves and the shoulder looks normal | Stop. **Urgent** (possible acute cuff tear). | [S1], [S2], [S4], [S5] |
| Signs of a joint infection: the shoulder is suddenly very painful **and** red or warm, or the client has a fever or feels unwell with shoulder pain | Stop. **Emergency.** | BESS: suspected joint infection needs same-day emergency referral [S1], [S2], [S4], immediate referral [S3]. See the note in section 4. |
| Arm pain together with chest pain, tightness, breathlessness, or feeling faint | Stop all exercise. **Emergency:** call emergency services. | [S10] |
| Pain at night that wakes the client, with stiffness that is not going away | Do not progress shoulder loading. **Routine.** | NHS frozen shoulder page: pain that can be worse at night and disturb sleep; see a GP if pain and stiffness do not go away [S7]. We found no BESS source that treats night pain alone as a red flag; we do not present it as one. |

## 4. Referral tiers

The trainer's job is to pick the tier and say it plainly. The trainer never decides what the problem is.

**Use the highest tier that any sign matches.** An emergency sign overrides every urgent or routine sign, and an urgent sign overrides every routine sign. Each presentation below appears in exactly one tier.

### Emergency: call emergency services (999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department now

| Criterion | Source |
|---|---|
| Chest pain that feels tight, crushing or squeezing, or chest pain that spreads to the arm, neck or jaw; severe difficulty breathing; the person collapses or does not respond normally | [S10] |
| After an injury (a fall, an impact, a joint that "popped out", a seizure or an electric shock), the client cannot move the arm, or the shoulder looks out of place or has changed shape. Do not move the arm or try to put it back. Do not let the client drive themselves. | [S6]; BESS: an unreduced dislocation needs same-day emergency referral [S1], [S2], [S4] |
| Signs of a joint infection: the shoulder is suddenly very painful **and** red or warm, **or** the client has a fever or feels unwell with shoulder pain | BESS: a suspected infected joint needs same-day emergency referral [S1], [S2], [S4]; any evidence of infection needs immediate referral [S3] |

Note on infection: the NHS shoulder pain page lists fever or feeling unwell, and an arm that is hot or cold to the touch, as urgent reasons to get help [S5]. Spindle's rule is deliberately stricter for fever or feeling unwell: a trainer cannot tell whether a joint is infected, and BESS asks for same-day emergency referral when infection is suspected. That stricter mapping is **Spindle policy**. A shoulder that feels hot or cold to the touch, or is badly swollen, without sudden severe pain, redness, fever or feeling unwell, stays urgent.

### Urgent: an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 today (or the local equivalent)

Only when no emergency sign is present.

| Criterion | Source |
|---|---|
| Sudden or very bad shoulder pain | [S5] |
| New loss of active movement without an injury: the client cannot lift or move the arm themselves, even if it can be moved for them | NHS: cannot move your arm [S5]; BESS: sudden loss of the ability to actively raise the arm [S4] |
| After an injury, pain and weakness, while the arm still moves and the shoulder looks normal (possible acute cuff tear). BESS asks for urgent specialist assessment at the next available clinic; its 2014 guide gives under 2 weeks. | [S1], [S2], [S4]; NHS: pain that started after an injury or accident [S5] |
| Any other new shoulder pain that started after an injury or accident, such as a fall | [S5] |
| The arm or shoulder has changed shape or is badly swollen, with no injury and no sign of infection | [S5] |
| Pins and needles that do not go away, or no feeling in the arm or shoulder | [S5] |
| The arm or shoulder feels hot or cold to the touch, with no sign of infection (no sudden severe pain with redness or warmth, no fever, not unwell) | [S5] |
| Severe pain in both shoulders | [S5] |
| A new lump, mass or swelling around the shoulder | BESS: suspected tumour follows the 2-week cancer referral pathway [S1], [S3], [S4] |
| New pain, swelling or stiffness in several joints at once | BESS: a rheumatological red flag that needs urgent referral [S1], [S2], [S4] |
| A child or adolescent with shoulder pain: urgent GP or NHS 111 the same day, for referral to trauma and orthopaedics; tell a parent or carer | BESS 2025: a red flag for urgent secondary-care referral to trauma and orthopaedics [S3] |

### Routine: a GP or physiotherapist (many areas allow self-referral to physiotherapy)

Only when no emergency or urgent sign is present.

| Criterion | Source |
|---|---|
| Shoulder pain that is getting worse. Refer now; do not wait for 2 weeks to pass. | [S5] |
| Shoulder pain that is not getting worse but has not improved after 2 weeks | [S5] |
| Moving the arm or shoulder is very difficult, but the client can still move it themselves | [S5] |
| Pain and stiffness that do not go away, including pain that is worse at night | [S7] |
| Pain that persists after the trainer has removed the provoking movements | [S5]; BESS pathways move to further assessment when symptoms do not respond to weeks of non-surgical care [S3], [S4] |

## 5. Where warnings sit

- **Beside each assessment and progression**, not only in the referral lesson. Every screen block is followed by a short "stop and refer if" block that links back to sections 3 and 4.
- Every loading progression names the signs that mean "do not progress".
- The referral lesson (lesson 8) teaches the tiers as a whole; earlier lessons repeat only the rules that apply at that point.
- Warning blocks are practice-based: they tell the trainer what to do and make no claim about the body. The sources here support the rules; they are not science claims in a lesson.

## 6. Quizzes

- The graded quiz includes adverse-case questions: given a short scenario, pick the correct action (continue, stop and refer routinely, urgent, or emergency).
- Every graded question binds an approved claim. Scenario questions that test the trainer's action against this document stay ungraded discussion until the claims they need are approved.
- Every scenario answer follows section 3: when pain appears, the answer stops shoulder loading for that session first.
- Wrong options never include a diagnostic action ("tell the client they have a cuff tear").

## 7. Review checklist

Each lesson review checks:

1. No diagnostic wording, and no screen that "clears" anyone.
2. Each assessment and progression has its stop and refer block.
3. No programming for an excluded population.
4. Every referral criterion in the lesson matches a row here, with the same tier.
5. Scenario answers stop loading for the session before suggesting any later change.
6. A missing or unapproved claim is never relabelled as practice: the statement is removed or rewritten as a coaching recommendation that says nothing about the body.
7. The chosen video segment and the context around it follow the same rules (no cure claims, no loading through warning signs).
8. Outcomes are not promised.

## 8. Known gaps

- **NICE CKS "Shoulder pain"** could not be read from outside the UK, so it is not cited. The BESS sources cover the same red flags.
- **Night pain** is supported only as a frozen-shoulder symptom [S7]. It is a routine referral, not a red flag, until a stronger source is read.
- **Neurological deficit** appears in NHS urgent advice [S5] but not in the BESS red-flag lists we read.
- **Post-surgical and neurological exclusions** are Spindle policy with the nearest support named.
- **Fever or feeling unwell as an emergency** is Spindle policy, stricter than the NHS page (section 4 note).
- **The NHS shoulder pain page** was last reviewed 22 May 2023 and is past its stated review date. Recheck it before launch.
- **The 2-week cuff-tear figure** comes from the 2014 commissioning guide [S4]. The 2015, 2021 and 2025 pathways say "urgent" without a number.
- **US and other regions:** the tiers name UK services because the sources are British. A lesson says "emergency services" and "an urgent appointment" so the action stays clear elsewhere.
